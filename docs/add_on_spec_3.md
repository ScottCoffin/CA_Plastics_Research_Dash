4. Precision-goal-driven evaluation labelling
Principle. Labels are bought to meet stated precision goals, not to fill a quota. The ~500 is a ceiling. Training (active learning) and evaluation are separate streams.
4.1 Two streams
•	Training stream (AL): uncertainty sampling, review-queue decisions and audits per ACTIVE_LEARNING.md (repo docs/ACTIVE_LEARNING.md); gold items are never used to select AL items.
•	Evaluation stream: an active-testing-style adaptive sample. Active testing selects which test points to label so evaluation is sample-efficient, and removes the bias that selective labelling introduces (the framework selects test points to label and removes the bias introduced by actively selecting labels, arXiv:2103.05331; see also active surrogate estimators for label-efficient evaluation, Kossen et al. 2022). Implementation here is the simple form: stratified sampling on frozen model scores with recorded inclusion probabilities and inverse-probability weights; estimates stay unbiased whatever the score quality, and a poor score only costs efficiency (general sampling statistics).
•	Stopping and certification logic is borrowed from active-learning screening: sample-based stopping rules built on quantile estimation (arXiv:2108.12746), a negative-binomial model of remaining relevant items (Howard et al. 2020), statistical stopping criteria (Callaghan and Müller-Hansen 2020). Those methods target finding relevant documents for reviews; here only the sampling and bound logic is reused (inference).

4.3 Design
1.	Strata (frozen before labelling): score band (calibrated probability bins) × block (California, global English, global non-English) × theme group (pollution-core, lifecycle-and-society, lens-candidate). Record model_version and score-snapshot hash.
2.	Round 0: ≥ 10 labels per non-empty stratum.
3.	Adaptive rounds of 25 labels: allocate in proportion to N_h·sqrt(p̂_h(1−p̂_h)) with Jeffreys-smoothed p̂_h and a positive floor per stratum; store incl_prob.
4.	Checkpoints after 100, 200, 300 and 350 global labels; early stopping only at a checkpoint; interim intervals unpublished (general practice against repeated-looks bias).
5.	Estimators: weighted precision and recall; stratified variance plus cluster bootstrap over author groups for the California block (B=1,000, fixed seed; 267 author groups, overlap 0; repo docs/S4_2_VERIFICATION.md).
6.	Spend-down: when P1 is met, remaining labels go to P3, then P4–P6, then the reserve.
7.	Label once: inclusion probabilities depend only on frozen strata, so the same labels can evaluate later models and queries by importance weighting (efficiency may drop).
8.	Leakage control: works labelled role=guide_example (hand-picked calibration cases, Section 5.4) are excluded from every stratum frame, the 150-work CA-eval set, the 80-work leak set, and every test or audit sample, so hand-picking cannot inflate or bias measured precision. Assert this in the splits-manifest check.
4.4 Ceilings
CA-eval 150 (fixed; test never used for thresholds; repo docs/ANNOTATION_WORKFLOW.md); global adaptive ≤ 200 (includes theme strata and the ≤ 40 lens-candidate stratum); language probe ≤ 120; reserve ≥ 30. Wilson 95% intervals for p̂ = 0.90, simple random sampling, no design effect (computed): n=100 → 0.826–0.945; 150 → 0.842–0.938; 200 → 0.851–0.934; 217 → 0.853–0.933; 350 → 0.864–0.927; 500 → 0.871–0.923.

4.7 Screening-classifier certification arithmetic (new; precedent only, A3-D22)
Ortiz-Balsero et al. (2026) triaged titles with a classifier before manual screening. Facts from the paper and its supplement (the supplement text was supplied by the owner):
•	The initial search identified 41,784 records and a February 2026 update added 3,618; after deduplication, LLM-assisted relevance screening and manual evaluation, 269 mammalian studies met the eligibility criteria and 28 addressed neurotoxicity. 7 The main text calls it a "customized LLM classifier fine-tuned on 338 manually labeled examples" (analyst reading of the main text 1).
•	Supplementary Text S2 describes it as a BERT-based neural classifier (BERT-base-uncased plus bidirectional LSTM and attention layers), trained on 551 labelled titles, 498 unique after deduplication (301 excluded, 197 included), split 338/100/60, with the decision threshold set to 0.22 instead of 0.50 to avoid false negatives. Reported recall was 1.00 on all splits; test specificity 0.927; test confusion matrix TN 38, FP 3, FN 0, TP 19 (Ortiz-Balsero et al. 2026, Supplementary Text S2).
•	The supplement itself notes that the test set contained only 19 relevant titles, and its text still holds unfilled template placeholders ("[n = 30]", "[range, e.g. 0.18–0.22]").
Arithmetic for the Atlas [computed]:
•	19 of 19 relevant test titles found → Wilson 95% lower bound on recall = 19/(19+1.96²) ≈ 0.832; one-sided 95% upper bound on the miss rate from zero misses in 19 = 1−0.05^(1/19) ≈ 14.6%.
•	Even across all 197 relevant labelled titles (training, validation and test pooled) the zero-miss bound would be ≈ 1.5%, but the threshold was tuned on these data, so that bound is optimistic.
•	Compare the Atlas P3 default: ≥150 labels with no hits for a ≤ 2% bound (Section 4.2). A recall of "1.00" on 19 positives does not meet that standard.
Rules adopted: (a) call such models "BERT-based classifier", not "LLM", in Atlas docs; (b) report the number of positives behind any recall claim and its Wilson bound; (c) never tune the threshold on the data used to certify it; (d) do not certify from placeholders.

5.4 Lifecycle-and-society taxonomy, including waste colonialism
What "lifecycle and society" means (A3-D13, confirmed). Works whose primary subject is plastics as a material, product, waste stream or policy object, and how these are produced, used, governed, managed, perceived or distributed across societies, rather than plastics' occurrence or effects as environmental pollutants (tier core) or their chemistry (tier chemicals-in-plastics).
Inspiration and limits. Discard Studies is described as "Social studies of waste, pollution & externalities" (discardstudies.com); the field looks at the wider social, economic, political, cultural and material systems that make waste and wasting what they are, rather than taking waste as its primary object (Discard Studies, "What is discard studies?", discardstudies.com/what-is-discard-studies), and a 2022 MIT Press book argues that systems maintain power by discarding certain people, places and things (Liboiron and Lepawsky 2022). Its site is a blog, not a set of scholarly works in OpenAlex, so it provides vocabulary and category inspiration, not a corpus. Use it only as a link-only entry in Related Resources (BQ-R2); do not copy its text. Its sidebar categories include Waste (196), Justice (EJ) (59), Difference: Class, Race, Gender (59), Labor (43), Government (59), Recycling (41), toxicity (31), e-waste (20) (discardstudies.com).

Waste colonialism — integration design (A3-D14)
What the literature and Discard Studies say (descriptive basis). The post defines waste colonialism as how waste and pollution are part of the domination of one group in their homeland by another; the term was first recorded in February 1989 at a Basel Convention working group, and its most common usage (by governments and NGOs) describes transboundary disposal of hazardous and toxic waste (Discard Studies 2018, "Waste colonialism", discardstudies.com/2018/11/01/waste-colonialism), while a land-based sense argues that colonialism is about access to Land and that regulatory limits to pollution make Land pollutable (same post). Sister terms are garbage imperialism, toxic colonialism, nuclear colonialism and toxic terrorism (same post). The post's headings include "Dispossession by Contamination" and a distinction between the capitalist relation and the colonial relation (same post). For plastics, a typology distinguishes classical waste colonialism, waste neocolonialism (direct export of toxic waste to former colonial states), indirect or "incidental" waste accumulation, and the criminal waste trade (Stoett 2024). Peryman et al. (2024) describe waste colonialism as the movement of waste from high-GDP to low-GDP countries, introduced in the Basel Convention (1989) and the Bamako Convention (1991), later extended to the colonization of plastic pollution science, and linked to "corporate imperialism". 23
Because usage varies, the Atlas does not collapse these senses.
